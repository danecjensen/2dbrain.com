### Surprising Facts About 1872 America from Jules Verne's *Around the World in 80 Days*

Jules Verne's depiction of America in these chapters draws from the post-Civil War era, capturing a wild, rapidly developing frontier nation that's vastly different from the regulated, tech-driven U.S. of 2025. Below, I've extracted key facts and details from the text that highlight historical realities or cultural norms of 1872, focusing on those likely to surprise modern readers due to changes in safety, society, technology, and law. These are presented chronologically by chapter for clarity, with explanations of why they'd be surprising today.

#### From Chapter XXV: San Francisco and Urban Life
- **Floating quays for shipping**: San Francisco's docks rose and fell with the tide to ease loading/unloading of massive international trade goods from places like Mexico, Chile, Peru, Brazil, Europe, Asia, and Pacific islands. *Surprising in 2025*: Modern ports use fixed infrastructure with cranes and automation; tidal quays sound precarious and low-tech, especially for a major hub handling global commerce.
- **Diverse population mix**: Streets filled with Americans, Europeans, Chinese, and Indians (likely Native Americans), alongside "Anglo-Saxon Gothic" churches, wooden/brick warehouses, omnibuses, and horse-cars. The Chinese quarter looked "imported from the Celestial Empire." *Surprising*: This early multiculturalism contrasts with today's integrated cities, but highlights 1872's raw immigration waves and segregation—Chinese laborers were often exploited during the Gold Rush aftermath.
- **Transformation from lawless outpost**: Just 23 years earlier (1849), San Francisco was a "paradise of outlaws" with bandits gambling gold dust while holding revolvers and bowie knives. By 1872, it was a "great commercial emporium" with silk-hatted businessmen on streets like Montgomery (comparable to Broadway or Regent Street). *Surprising*: The speed of urbanization from Wild West chaos to sophisticated trade center in under a generation; today, such shifts take decades with heavy regulation.
- **Hotel bars as free buffets**: Ground-floor bars offered dried beef, oyster soup, biscuits, and cheese for free to passers-by, with payment only for alcohol like ale, porter, or sherry. Served by "negroes of darkest hue" on tiny plates. *Surprising*: No modern equivalent to this "very American" open-access model; it'd be a health code violation and business loss today, plus the casual racial description reflects era norms.
- **Casual gun purchases for travel**: Passepartout buys Enfield rifles and Colt revolvers due to stories of Sioux and Pawnee train attacks, seen as a normal precaution. *Surprising*: In 2025, buying firearms impulsively for travel sounds like frontier vigilantism; strict gun laws and TSA rules make this unthinkable.
- **Violent political rallies**: A massive crowd in Montgomery Street for an election turned into a riot with fists, sticks, boots, revolvers, and canes—over electing a justice of the peace (not a high office like governor). Banners torn, hats crushed, and flanks attacked like a battle. *Surprising*: Elections today are orderly with security; this level of public brawling evokes January 6 but as routine, highlighting 1872's unstable democracy post-Reconstruction.
- **Duels as honor resolution**: After the riot, Fogg vows to return to America to duel Colonel Proctor for a punch, as "it would not be right for an Englishman to permit himself to be treated that way." *Surprising*: Duels were illegal but culturally accepted; in 2025, this macho code of honor is archaic, replaced by lawsuits or therapy.

#### From Chapter XXVI: Transcontinental Railroad Travel
- **Rapid railroad construction**: The Pacific Railroad (Central + Union Pacific) spanned 3,786 miles from ocean to ocean, built starting 1862 despite Southern opposition. It grew 1.5 miles/day on prairies, with locomotives laying rails ahead of themselves. Crossed Indian-infested territories colonized by Mormons after 1845 expulsion from Illinois. *Surprising*: Built in ~7 years amid Civil War fallout; today's infrastructure projects drag on for decades with environmental reviews and bureaucracy.
- **Travel time revolution**: New York to San Francisco took 7 days by rail vs. 6 months previously. *Surprising*: In 2025, it's a 5-6 hour flight; but 1872's "speed" was groundbreaking, yet slow and perilous compared to modern transport.
- **Train car amenities**: Long omnibus-like cars with aisle seats, connecting platforms, saloons, restaurants, smoking cars, and vendors selling books, food, drinks, cigars. At night, seats converted to curtained berths with clean sheets and soft pillows. *Surprising*: Like a mobile hotel, but no privacy or safety features; today's trains/planes have tech like Wi-Fi, yet this sounds luxurious for the era but rudimentary now.
- **Buffalo herds blocking trains**: Massive herds (10-12,000 animals) could halt trains for hours, forming "a torrent of living flesh" that locomotives couldn't plow through without derailing. *Surprising*: Wildlife disruptions on this scale are extinct; bison populations were decimated soon after, and modern rail avoids such issues with fencing and wildlife corridors.
- **Route and terrain**: Train followed Platte River, crossed Wahsatch Mountains, skirted Great Salt Lake, with minimal bridges/tunnels—winding around mountains at grades under 112 ft/mile. Passed through Nevada's Humboldt Range and vast prairies. *Surprising*: This engineering feat with basic tools; today, high-speed rail tunnels through mountains, and the West is developed, not "infested by Indians and wild beasts."

#### From Chapter XXVII: Mormonism and Utah
- **Mormon polygamy as norm**: Mormons practiced polygamy (foundation of their faith), with women eager to marry for religious salvation (unmarried women denied "highest joys" in afterlife). One man guiding multiple wives seen as burdensome. *Surprising*: Polygamy was outlawed federally by 1890; in 2025, it's illegal and taboo, contrasting with modern emphasis on equality and consent.
- **Persecution and expansion**: Mormons fled persecutions in Vermont, Illinois, Ohio, Missouri; founded Nauvoo (25,000 people) and Salt Lake City (fertile colony on emigrant routes). U.S. government imprisoned Brigham Young for rebellion/polygamy, seizing Utah. *Surprising*: This religious exodus and federal crackdown echoes refugee crises, but Mormons as "independent fanatics" resisting Congress highlights 1872's religious tensions vs. today's protections.
- **Salt Lake City layout**: Built on a checkerboard with right angles, symmetry-obsessed Anglo-Saxon style; included prophet's mansion, courthouse, arsenal, few churches, clay/pebble walls, and women in black silk or Indian fashion. *Surprising*: The rigid grid persists, but the "sombre sadness" and polygamous households feel alien; modern Utah is mainstream, not a theocratic outpost.
- **Missionary lectures on trains**: Elder Hitch preached Mormon history (from Biblical times, Joseph Smith's 1825 revelations, persecutions, martyrdoms) in a car, attracting curious passengers. *Surprising*: Proselytizing in public transport today would be awkward or prohibited; reflects 1872's fluid social spaces and religious fervor.

#### From Chapter XXVIII: Rocky Mountains and Risks
- **Government subsidies for tough terrain**: Engineers got $48,000/mile in mountains vs. $16,000 on plains, avoiding nature by curving around rocks instead of tunneling. *Surprising*: Massive public funding for private rail (corruption scandals ensued); today's projects face endless debates over costs and environmental impact.
- **Risky bridge crossing**: Train backed up, then rushed at 100 mph over a shaky suspension bridge, collapsing it afterward. Passengers cheered the "American" daring. *Surprising*: Sheer recklessness—no safety inspections or evacuations; in 2025, this would be a catastrophe with lawsuits, not a heroic tale.

#### From Chapter XXIX: Plains and Conflicts
- **Sioux train attacks**: Bands of 100 Sioux boarded moving trains like circus performers, fought passengers hand-to-hand, pillaged baggage, and tried stopping the engine. Passengers armed with revolvers defended barricaded cars. *Surprising*: Native American resistance to railroads invading lands; today, trains are secure, and such violence is historical fiction, not routine.
- **Duels on trains**: Fogg and Proctor arrange a revolver duel in a cleared car, interrupted by the attack. *Surprising*: Casual gunfights over card games; modern travel bans weapons, and disputes go to authorities.
- **Inauguration festivities**: Union Pacific's 1867 opening at Julesburg included Indian battle reenactments, fireworks, and on-site newspaper printing. *Surprising*: Rail as "mighty instrument of progress" amid deserts; evokes manifest destiny, now critiqued for displacing indigenous peoples.

#### From Chapter XXX: Aftermath and Rescue
- **Volunteer rescues from forts**: Fort Kearney's captain sends 30 soldiers to rescue captives from Sioux, offering $5,000 reward. *Surprising*: Military aiding civilians against "Indians"; in 2025, this is SWAT or FBI work, not ad-hoc posses.
- **Detached locomotive returns**: Engineer reverses alone to fetch help after Sioux hijacking. *Surprising*: No communication tech like radios; reliance on ingenuity in isolation.
- **Delayed trains as fatal**: Missing a connection by hours could doom a journey, with next trains daily. *Surprising*: No apps or backups; 1872 travel was unforgiving vs. today's redundancies.

These details paint 1872 America as a booming but brutal place—guns everywhere, nature untamed, society in flux—far from the safety-netted, digital world of 2025. Verne romanticizes it, but the perils underscore how much has changed in infrastructure, civil rights, and daily risks.
